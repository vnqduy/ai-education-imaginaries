# Core papers for the manuscript

Selected for theoretical and methodological relevance, not as a systematic review. New recommendations were checked against publisher abstracts, publication records and author/institutional records; full texts were not required. Suggested uses below are editorial judgments.

1. **Rahm, Lina; Rahm-Skågeby, Jörgen (2023). [Imaginaries and problematisations: A heuristic lens in the age of artificial intelligence in education](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13319).** *British Journal of Educational Technology*. Core theoretical bridge: imaginaries and problematisations.

2. **Rahm, Lina (2023). [Educational imaginaries: Governance at the intersection of technology and education](https://doi.org/10.1080/02680939.2021.1970233).** *Journal of Education Policy*. Educational governance at the intersection of technological futures and educational purposes.

3. **Brause, Saba Rebecca; Schäfer, Mike S.; Katzenbach, Christian; Mao, Yishu; Richter, Vanessa; Zeng, Jing (2025). [Sociotechnical imaginaries and public communication: Analytical framework and empirical illustration using the case of artificial intelligence](https://doi.org/10.1177/13548565251338192).** *Convergence: The International Journal of Research into New Media Technologies*. Core analytical framework for public articulation and attributed relationships.

4. **Linderoth, Cornelia; Hultén, Magnus; Stenliden, Linnéa (2024). [Competing visions of artificial intelligence in education—A heuristic analysis on sociotechnical imaginaries and problematizations in policy guidelines](https://doi.org/10.1177/14782103241228900).** *Policy Futures in Education*. Closest policy-based comparison linking educational AI visions and problem representations.

5. **Horvath, Kenneth; Frei, Andrea Isabel; Steinberg, Mario (2025). [How do problematisations “materialise”? A neopragmatic perspective on the (in-)visibilisation of justice concerns in AIED](https://doi.org/10.1080/01425692.2025.2531262).** *British Journal of Sociology of Education*. Priority addition: problematisations moving into EdTech development and the visibility of justice concerns.

6. **Shi, Lulu P.; Selwyn, Neil; Eynon, Rebecca; Knox, Jeremy (2026). [Toward a sociology of education and artificial intelligence](https://doi.org/10.1080/01425692.2026.2686013).** *British Journal of Sociology of Education*. Sociological framing of power, educational purposes and alternatives; an agenda-setting introduction.

7. **Pastore, Sara (2025). [‘The Classroom Robot’, by ChatGPT: Unpacking the Imaginary of the AIED Classroom Through an AI-Generated Story](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42438-025-00564-x).** *Postdigital Science and Education*. Priority addition: promises and fears within an imagined automated classroom.

8. **Hrastinski, Stefan; Olofsson, Anders D.; Arkenback, Charlotte; Ekström, Sara; Ericsson, Elin; Fransson, Göran; Jaldemark, Jimmy; Ryberg, Thomas; Öberg, Lena-Maria; Fuentes, Ana; Gustafsson, Ulrika; Humble, Niklas; Mozelius, Peter; Sundgren, Marcus; Utterberg, Marie (2019). [Critical imaginaries and reflections on artificial intelligence and robots in postdigital K-12 education](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42438-019-00046-x).** *Postdigital Science and Education*. Teacher versus researcher/developer imaginaries; useful for actor distinctions.

9. **Cerratto Pargman, Teresa; Lindberg, Ylva; Buch, Anders (2023). [Automation Is Coming! Exploring Future(s)-Oriented Methods in Education](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42438-022-00349-6).** *Postdigital Science and Education*. Future-oriented methods; situate the news approach against elicited speculative accounts.

10. **Rahm, Lina (2023). [Education, automation and AI: A genealogy of alternative futures](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439884.2021.1977948).** *Learning, Media and Technology*. Historical relation between automation and demands for educational preparation.

11. **Macgilchrist, Felicitas (2019). [Cruel optimism in edtech: When the digital data practices of educational technology providers inadvertently hinder educational equity](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439884.2018.1556217).** *Learning, Media and Technology*. Educational equity commitments and their tension with EdTech data practices.

12. **Knox, Jeremy; Williamson, Ben; Bayne, Sian (2020). [Machine behaviourism: Future visions of ‘learnification’ and ‘datafication’ across humans and digital technologies](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439884.2019.1623251).** *Learning, Media and Technology*. Learner autonomy, participation and behavioural governance.

13. **Gidiotis, Iosif; Hrastinski, Stefan (2026). [Imagining the future of artificial intelligence in education: A review of social science fiction](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439884.2024.2365829).** *Learning, Media and Technology*. Education-fiction review; neighbouring approach to AI futures, not equivalent to news analysis.

14. **Huang, Xi; Gadavanij, Savitri (2025). [Power and marginalization in discourse on AI in education (AIEd): Social actors’ representation in China Daily (2018–2023)](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-025-04621-5).** *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*. Direct empirical comparator for actor representation in educational AI news.

15. **Brause, Saba Rebecca; Yang, Heng; Schäfer, Mike S.; Zeng, Jing (2024). [News media imaginaries of artificial intelligence in healthcare: A qualitative analysis across China, Germany, and the United States](https://doi.org/10.1177/27523543241300185).** *Emerging Media*. Role-based comparative news analysis; the closest methodological predecessor.

Publication-year note: Cerratto Pargman et al. appeared online in 2022 and in volume 5 (2023); Gidiotis and Hrastinski appeared online in 2024 and in volume 51 (2026). The bibliography uses issue years for these articles.

16. **Chung, Hiu-Fung (2026). [Betting on (un)certain futures: Sociotechnical imaginaries of AI and varieties of techno-developmentalism in Asia](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2025.2535427).** *Information, Communication & Society*, 29(3), 909–926. Policy comparison of Singapore, Taiwan and Hong Kong: shared techno-developmental framing with distinct institutional and geopolitical orientations. Publisher abstract and publication metadata checked; full text not accessed. Published online in 2025; bibliography uses the 2026 issue year.


17. **Ferrante, Patricia et al. (2024). [In/equalities in digital education policy – sociotechnical imaginaries from three world regions](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439884.2023.2237870).** *Learning, Media and Technology*, 49(1), 122–132. Methodological precedent combining sociotechnical imaginaries and Bacchi’s WPR in digital education policy analysis across six countries. Use with Linderoth to establish the combined approach and the policy-document tradition. The discussion of unproblematised ecological costs informs questions about the boundaries of educational AI justifications; it does not establish Vietnamese silences.

18. **Schiff, Daniel (2022). [Education for AI, not AI for Education: The Role of Education and Ethics in National AI Policy Strategies](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40593-021-00270-2).** *International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education*, 32(3), 527–563. Use in Discussion to interpret the prominence of Learning Partner and AI Literacy. Keep the distinction separate from the role taxonomy: literacy includes critical and responsible participation as well as workforce preparation.

## Literature-informed questions about underdeveloped problems — 22 October 2026

These are analytical questions for checking source passages, not new codes, confirmed corpus-wide absences, or evidence of intentional state suppression. The education-focused retrieval and future-oriented eligibility rules limit which wider AI debates can be expected here. Account summaries cannot establish absence: raw sources include substantive considerations omitted from those summaries, and retained web text also includes navigation and related-story material.

- **Ecological costs of educational AI infrastructure.** Basis: Rahm (2023), *Educational imaginaries*, explicitly addresses hidden social and ecological costs; Ferrante et al. discuss ecological costs left unproblematised in development-oriented digital education policy. Screened passages include GDN25_008 and TNI26_008 presenting AI applications for environmental benefits, and NHD26_014 presenting water-efficient cooling as part of desirable university research infrastructure. The latter is a counterexample to complete environmental absence. A defensible question is whether AI’s own material costs become grounds for reconsidering educational expansion, rather than appearing only as manageable infrastructure features or problems AI can solve. The manuscript currently makes a bounded reading of NHD26_014, not a corpus prevalence claim.

- **Commercial appropriation and control of educational data.** Basis: Nemorin et al. (2023) discuss learners as data sources, extraction, and limited agency; Macgilchrist (2019) examines the tensions among equity, privacy, ownership, and provider data practices. Distinguish privacy/security warnings from questions about who gains value from data and who can determine its uses. NHD25_005 discusses privacy and national control, so neither privacy nor control can be described as simply absent. Search matches for “khai thác dữ liệu” often concern curricular data mining or beneficial analysis, not exploitation. Further contextual reading is needed before describing commercial appropriation as underdeveloped across coverage.

- **Discriminatory effects and unequal educational outcomes.** Basis: Ferrante et al. distinguish participation-oriented representations from inequalities in outcomes; Macgilchrist (2019) shows why equitable aspirations do not settle data practices. Raw sources NHD24_002, GDN26_042, BCP26_012 and DTR26_009 include discrimination, algorithmic bias or bias evaluation. These are counterexamples to a claim of absence. Examine whether bias becomes a matter of user literacy, provider accountability, unequal assessment, or redress; do not treat a generic ethics mention as evidence that each has been examined.

- **Authority to accept, contest, or refuse deployment.** Basis: Nemorin et al. discuss learner agency and control of personal data; Knox et al. (2020) address behavioural governance. GDN25_034 includes voluntary parental consent for optional activities; VNP26_009, VTV26_009 and TTO26_030 report an institutional opt-in response to automatic AI activation. Consent is therefore present. A further question is whose authority is recognised: schools, parents, teachers, or learners, and whether the decision concerns a particular product or the wider educational arrangement. Low learner speaker prevalence alone cannot answer this.

- **Labour sustaining AI production.** Distinguish teacher workload and occupational displacement, which the manuscript already examines, from annotation, moderation, and other labour behind AI systems. The initial screen did not identify explicit labour-exploitation accounts, but this category is particularly vulnerable to retrieval scope and requires stronger literature grounding and broader contextual checks before becoming a finding.

Recent policy context checked on 22 October 2026 includes the August 2026 national AI human-resource programme (Decision 1528/QĐ-TTg), school implementation guidance (Dispatch 5588/BGDĐT-GDPT), and September reporting of implementation in Hanoi. The retained main corpus ends on 23 August 2026. Later policy reports contextualise the discussion and must not be treated as additional sampled articles or as proof that news caused implementation.
